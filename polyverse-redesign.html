Overview

Polyverse is an AI-powered city exploration platform designed for moments when people want to go out but do not yet know what they want to do. It translates context such as time, location, mood, and company into possibilities users can evaluate and act on.

As the only product designer, I led the product from strategy and research through interaction design, AI experience design, prototyping, validation, and the design-system foundation—turning an open-ended opportunity into a coherent product direction and testable experience.

Role

Lead Product Designer

Owned product strategy and stayed hands-on across research, AI interaction, prototyping, testing, and visual execution.

Scope

0→1 AI Exploration Experience

Defined the core journey, recommendation model, refinement behavior, scalable system, and beta product direction.

Team

Cross-functional Product Team

Partnered closely with one founder, three engineers, marketing, and community contributors.

/ 02

Opportunity

Urban Exploration Gap

People often begin city exploration with a feeling rather than a destination: a free hour, a desire for somewhere quiet, or simply the urge to get out. Most tools assume that users already know what they want and optimize for search, reviews, planning, or booking.

Market mapping revealed a gap between inspiration and action. Existing platforms helped people collect ideas, but few supported lightweight, context-aware decisions in the moment.

Product Opportunity

The opportunity was not another destination directory. Polyverse could connect discovery, evaluation, and action in one continuous journey—helping people understand what was realistically possible now instead of forcing them to assemble an answer across maps, reviews, social feeds, and messages.

/ 03

Research

Research Approach

I combined interviews, a survey of 50 urban Gen Z participants, competitive research, usability testing, and observation. Rather than treating individual feature requests as requirements, I looked for recurring behavior patterns across the end-to-end journey.

The research showed that exploration frequently began with loose situational intent, while planning required people to compare fragmented information across several products.

Key Insights

Two patterns changed the direction of the product. First, exploration starts with a situation—time, mood, location, or company—not a predefined destination. Second, users were not missing options; they were missing a coherent way to decide which option fit the moment.

/ 04

Design Approach

Product Vision

Polyverse would turn open-ended exploration into real-world action. Instead of requiring users to formulate the perfect search, the product would accept loose intent, assemble relevant context, and help people progressively recognize what felt right.

Experience Principles

The research translated into two experience commitments that guided the sequence of the work. The first addressed how exploration begins; the second addressed how planning breaks apart.

AI Principles

AI should reduce the work of forming and refining a decision, not claim authority over the final choice. Recommendations needed to explain why they fit, preserve multiple possibilities, and make feedback part of the same conversation.

/ 05

Core Journey

Connecting the Planning Journey

I first addressed the more direct research problem: planning was fragmented across maps, reviews, social media, and messages. I organized the core experience as one continuous journey so users could move from loose intent to real-world action without rebuilding context elsewhere.

After connecting discovery, evaluation, and action, I ran moderated usability tests to understand whether the integrated flow reduced planning friction—not only the number of tools people used.

The Remaining Confidence Gap

Testing showed that the integrated journey largely solved the switching problem. But it exposed a deeper issue: even when recommendations looked relevant, users still hesitated because they could not tell whether an option actually fit their current situation.

/ 06

Exploration & Validation

From Relevance to Actionability

Observed confidence gap: The connected journey reduced switching, but one pattern remained: even when recommendations looked relevant, users were still not confident enough to decide where to go.

First hypothesis: Maybe users hesitated because the results simply were not specific enough. To test that assumption, I worked with the team to introduce more categories, contextual signals, and advanced filter combinations.

What testing revealed: The additional filters narrowed results exactly as intended, but they did not solve the real problem. Users could define a more precise search and still could not tell which option fit their actual situation.

Reframe: I shifted the product question from “Is this relevant?” to “Is this something the user can realistically do right now?” Contextual actionability became the recommendation principle behind the next iteration.

Iteration: I replaced filter configuration with conversational context. Users could describe their situation naturally while the system surfaced a small set of activities, explained why each fit, and reduced the effort required to move forward.

Designing for Progressive Refinement

Pattern from the next test: After validating the contextual recommendation experience, I ran another usability study. Users understood and trusted the recommendations more quickly, yet some still closed the first set, rewrote their description, and started again—not because the options were bad, but because describing a personal situation perfectly in one prompt was difficult.

Trade-off: The team considered improving the model until it could produce the perfect answer in one shot. I reframed the problem as an interaction gap: when the first answer was close but imperfect, users had no natural way to build on it.

Validation: I prototyped an interaction in which every recommendation became the starting point for the next conversation. Users could react to the current result, preserve what worked, add context, and update the recommendation without losing progress.

/ 07

Final Design

Core Exploration Experience

The final journey connects discovery, evaluation, refinement, and action. Users begin with loose intent, review a focused set of possibilities, understand why each fits, refine the recommendation, and move directly into saving, sharing, or navigation.

Figo: AI Exploration Companion

I designed Figo as the personality layer for the AI experience. Inspired by New York pigeons, Figo behaves like a local insider rather than a generic chatbot—introducing possibilities, guiding refinement, and making the intelligence feel approachable and memorable.

Design System

I built the foundations and reusable UI layer from scratch using Figma Variables, component variants, and Auto Layout. Color, typography, spacing, iconography, states, and component behavior were documented as a shared language between design and engineering.

The system helped the team implement new experiences faster, reduce repeated UI decisions, and preserve consistency as the beta product continued to evolve.

/ 08

Impact

The work established a validated product direction for contextual city exploration, connecting fragmented planning into one journey and giving users a clearer path from open-ended intent to confident real-world action.

/ 09

Reflection

Polyverse reinforced that the most valuable product decisions often come from challenging a reasonable assumption rather than polishing its first solution.

Design the right problem:
Better filters could narrow results, but reframing the problem around actionability created the meaningful shift.

Use AI to support judgment:
The strongest experience did not deliver one perfect answer; it helped people refine their own decision.

Build systems for continued learning:
A shared interaction and component foundation allowed product evidence to translate into faster, more coherent iteration.
